# People Frameworks

Mental models and tools for building and leading teams. Reference when hiring, managing contractors, designing roles, or building culture.

---

## Hiring & Selection

**The Scorecard Method.** Developed by Geoff Smart in *Who*, the scorecard is a document you create before sourcing any candidate. It contains three sections: the mission of the role (a single sentence describing why this role exists), outcomes (three to five measurable results the person must deliver in the first twelve months), and competencies (the behaviours and skills required to achieve those outcomes). The scorecard replaces the traditional job description, which tends to be a laundry list of qualifications. Instead, it functions as a performance contract — if the person delivers these outcomes, they have succeeded. When evaluating candidates, you score them against the scorecard, not against each other or against your gut feeling. This eliminates the most common hiring failure mode: selecting someone who "feels right" but has no demonstrated ability to deliver what the role actually requires. Use for: any hiring decision, whether full-time, contractor, or fractional. Write the scorecard before you write the job ad. Share it with the candidate so expectations are explicit from day one.

**Structured Behavioural Interviews.** Unstructured interviews — "tell me about yourself," vibes-based conversation — have near-zero predictive validity. Structured interviews use the same questions for every candidate, scored against a rubric tied to the scorecard. The format is behavioural: "Tell me about a specific time when you [competency]. What was the situation, what did you do, and what was the result?" You are listening for concrete past behaviour, not hypothetical future promises. Each interviewer scores independently before any debrief to avoid anchoring bias. For a solo founder, this means writing five to seven questions before the first interview, scoring each answer from one to five against predefined criteria, and resisting the urge to go off-script when someone is charming. Charm is not a competency unless the role requires sales. Use for: every interview, even informal coffee chats with potential contractors. The structure protects you from your own biases.

**Backdoor Reference Checks.** Smart's method calls for collecting references not just from the names a candidate provides (who will always be positive) but from people you find through your own network. Ask the candidate: "Who was your manager at [company]? We will be reaching out to them." This single question changes the dynamic — candidates who have something to hide will often self-select out. When conducting the reference call, use a structured script: "What were [name]'s biggest strengths? What were their biggest areas for improvement? On a scale of one to ten, how would you rate their overall performance? Would you enthusiastically rehire them?" The "enthusiastically rehire" question is the most revealing — anything less than a strong yes is a data point worth weighting heavily. In the Australian market, some referees are cautious due to liability concerns, so frame questions around observable behaviour rather than character judgments. Use for: final-stage hiring decisions. Never skip this step, even when you are excited about a candidate. Especially when you are excited about a candidate.

**Paid Trial Projects.** Before making a commitment — whether a full-time offer or a long-term contractor engagement — design a paid trial that simulates real work. The trial should be two to five days, use a genuine problem from your business (not a toy exercise), and be compensated at the person's normal rate. You are evaluating: quality of output, communication style, ability to work autonomously, how they handle ambiguity, and whether they ask good questions. The trial also lets the candidate evaluate you — which is important because the best people have options and are assessing whether your operating environment is one they want to be in. In Australia, be mindful of sham contracting rules: a trial should be genuinely short-term and project-based, not an unpaid or underpaid probation period disguised as freelance work. Use for: any engagement where you are committing significant time or money. The cost of a bad hire is ten to twenty times the cost of a trial project.

## Leadership & Management

**Radical Candor.** Kim Scott's framework plots two dimensions: care personally (genuine investment in someone's growth and wellbeing) and challenge directly (willingness to tell hard truths about performance and behaviour). The magic quadrant is where both are high — you care enough to be honest and you are honest enough to drive improvement. The three failure modes are: ruinous empathy (high care, low challenge — you are too "nice" to give critical feedback, so the person never improves), obnoxious aggression (low care, high challenge — you are blunt but the person feels attacked, so they shut down), and manipulative insincerity (low on both — you say what people want to hear while privately judging them). For a solo founder managing contractors or early hires, the most common trap is ruinous empathy: you need the person, so you avoid hard conversations, and quality slowly degrades. The antidote is to give feedback within 48 hours of the event, in private, focused on behaviour not character, and paired with a specific suggestion for what to do differently. Use for: every feedback conversation, every 1:1, every moment where you notice something is not meeting the bar and you are tempted to let it slide.

**Situational Leadership (Hersey & Blanchard).** Not everyone needs the same management style, and the same person needs different styles at different times. The model maps two variables — the person's competence and their commitment — to four leadership approaches: directing (high guidance, low support — for someone new to a task), coaching (high guidance, high support — for someone learning but struggling), supporting (low guidance, high support — for someone capable but lacking confidence), and delegating (low guidance, low support — for someone who is both competent and motivated). The key insight for a founder is that you must diagnose each person and each task separately. A brilliant engineer might need "delegating" on code but "directing" on client communication. When you add your first people, start by identifying where each person sits on each major responsibility, and adjust your involvement accordingly. The mistake most founders make is defaulting to either micromanagement (directing everything) or abdication (delegating everything). Neither works. Use for: deciding how much to involve yourself in someone's work, onboarding new contractors, calibrating your management intensity.

**Effective 1:1s.** The one-on-one meeting is the single highest-leverage management ritual. It is the employee's or contractor's meeting, not yours — they set the agenda. Your job is to listen, remove blockers, and provide coaching. A good cadence is weekly for new relationships (first three months) and fortnightly once trust and rhythm are established. The structure: ten minutes on their priorities and blockers, ten minutes on your feedback and context-sharing, ten minutes on their development and career. Never cancel 1:1s — it signals that the person's growth is less important than whatever else is on your calendar. For async or contractor relationships where a synchronous meeting is impractical, replace the 1:1 with a structured written check-in: "What did you ship this week? What is blocking you? What decision do you need from me?" The written format has the added benefit of creating a paper trail of progress and issues. Use for: every ongoing people relationship, adjusted in format and frequency to the context.

**The Delegation Ladder.** Not all delegation is equal. The ladder runs from level one ("do exactly what I say") to level five ("own this entirely and tell me what you decided afterward"). Most founders oscillate between levels one and five — micromanaging on some tasks and abdicating on others — without ever using the middle rungs. The middle is where leverage lives: level three is "research options and recommend one; I will decide" and level four is "decide and act, but tell me what you did." As trust builds, you move people up the ladder. As a solo builder, your goal is to get every recurring task to level four or five as quickly as possible. If a task stays at level one or two for more than a month, either the person is wrong for the role or you have not invested enough in coaching them up. Use for: briefing new work, defining the level of autonomy for each project, identifying where you are bottlenecking your own team.

## Culture & Values

**Culture as Behaviour, Not Words.** A common founder mistake is to write a list of values on a Notion page and believe culture is handled. Culture is not what you say — it is the collection of behaviours you reward, tolerate, and punish. If you say you value "ownership" but you never let anyone make a decision without your approval, your actual culture is one of control. If you say you value "quality" but you ship half-finished work to meet arbitrary deadlines, your actual culture is one of speed over craft. The diagnostic question is simple: look at the last three decisions you made about people (who you hired, who you praised, what you let slide) — those decisions are your culture. For a solo builder adding first people, this means every action in the first six months is a cultural precedent. There is no "we will fix culture later." Use for: auditing whether your stated values match your actual behaviour, onboarding new people with honest expectations.

**Values as Hiring and Firing Filters.** Your values should be specific enough to be controversial — "we value honesty" is not a value because no one would claim to value dishonesty. A real value is something where the opposite is also a legitimate choice. "We value speed over polish" is a real value because some great companies choose the opposite. Once you have three to five real values, use them as literal filters in hiring (does this person demonstrably live this value?) and in performance conversations (is this person violating a value, and is that a coachable gap or a fundamental misalignment?). Amazon's leadership principles work because they are specific, behavioural, and used in every hiring loop. You do not need fourteen principles — three sharp ones are enough for a small team. Use for: writing scorecards, conducting interviews, making tough calls about whether someone is the right fit.

**Psychological Safety (Amy Edmondson).** Edmondson's research at Harvard shows that the highest-performing teams are not the ones that make the fewest mistakes — they are the ones where people feel safe to admit mistakes, ask questions, and challenge ideas without fear of punishment. In practice, this means the leader must go first: share your own mistakes, ask "what am I missing?" in every discussion, and respond to bad news with curiosity rather than blame. For a tiny team, psychological safety is both easier (fewer people, more intimacy) and harder (every interaction is high-stakes because there is nowhere to hide). The founder sets the ceiling for safety: if you react defensively to feedback once, people will never give you honest feedback again. Use for: designing team rituals, responding to mistakes and missed targets, building the kind of trust that enables radical candor.

**The Keeper Test (Netflix).** Reed Hastings frames it as: "If someone on your team told you they were leaving for a similar role at another company, would you fight hard to keep them — or would you feel secretly relieved?" If the answer is relief, act now. Give the person a generous severance (Netflix famously pays well above statutory minimums) and free the seat for someone you would fight for. In Australia, you must navigate the Fair Work Act's unfair dismissal provisions, which apply after the minimum employment period (six months for businesses with fifteen or more employees, twelve months for small businesses). This makes probation periods and clear performance documentation essential. The keeper test is not about being ruthless — it is about respecting both the person (who deserves to be somewhere they are valued) and the team (which deserves colleagues who raise the bar). Use for: quarterly team review, contract renewal decisions, honest self-assessment of every working relationship.

## Compensation & Structure

**Salary Bands and Market Positioning.** Define compensation ranges for each role before you negotiate with any individual. A band has a minimum, midpoint, and maximum. The midpoint represents the market rate for a fully competent person in the role. New hires start at or below midpoint; exceptional performers can grow to the top of the band without needing a title change. In Australia, check the Award system — many roles are covered by a Modern Award under the Fair Work Act, which sets minimum pay rates, overtime, and penalty rates. Even if you are hiring above-Award, you must ensure your package meets or exceeds the relevant Award floor. Use salary surveys (Hays, Robert Half, Seek salary data) to benchmark, and update bands annually. Transparency about bands — sharing them in job ads — is increasingly expected in the Australian market and is a competitive advantage for small companies competing against corporates for talent. Use for: setting compensation for new roles, managing pay equity as your team grows, preparing for salary negotiation conversations.

**Superannuation and Australian Entitlements.** If you engage someone as an employee in Australia, you are legally required to pay superannuation at the current Superannuation Guarantee rate (11.5% as of 2025-26, legislated to rise to 12% by 2025-26) on top of their ordinary time earnings. You must also provide the National Employment Standards (NES), which include four weeks paid annual leave, ten days paid personal/carer's leave, parental leave, and public holidays. For contractors, super is generally not required — but if the ATO determines the contractor is an "employee for super purposes" (primarily working for you, performing work personally, being paid for labour rather than a result), you may owe super anyway. The safe approach: if engaging someone long-term, get advice from an employment lawyer or accountant on classification before you start. The cost of getting this wrong includes back-payment of super plus penalties and interest via the Super Guarantee Charge. Use for: budgeting total employment cost (add roughly 30-40% on top of base salary for super, leave, payroll tax, workers comp, and overheads), structuring contractor versus employee engagements.

**Equity and Upside Sharing for Early Contributors.** In Australia, employee share schemes (ESS) are governed by Division 83A of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997. The rules can create favourable tax treatment if structured correctly — particularly the startup concession, which allows employees of eligible startups to defer tax on share scheme interests until a liquidity event (up to fifteen years). To qualify, the company must have been incorporated for less than ten years, have an aggregated turnover of less than $50 million, and not be listed on a stock exchange. If you are a solo builder considering giving equity to early hires or key contractors, structure it as options with a vesting schedule (four years with a one-year cliff is standard). For contractors, equity is trickier — they are typically not eligible for ESS concessions, so consider a phantom equity or profit-sharing arrangement instead. Always get legal advice; the intersection of employment law, tax law, and corporate law here is genuinely complex. Use for: designing compensation packages for early hires, deciding whether and how to share upside with key contributors.

**Contractor Rate Setting.** When engaging contractors, the rate should reflect that they bear their own super, insurance, equipment, leave (unpaid), and business costs. A rough rule of thumb: a contractor's day rate should be approximately 1.5 to 2 times what the equivalent employee would earn on a per-day basis. So if an employee role pays $150,000 per year (roughly $600 per day), the equivalent contractor rate is $900 to $1,200 per day. Negotiate based on scope and duration: longer engagements can justify a lower rate (more certainty for the contractor), while short, specialist engagements command a premium. Always agree rates in writing before work begins, and be explicit about what is included — is the rate for deliverables or for time? Milestone-based pricing (fixed price for a defined deliverable) transfers risk to the contractor and incentivises efficiency; time-based pricing (day rate or hourly) keeps flexibility but requires you to manage scope. Use for: setting rates for new contractor engagements, evaluating whether a contractor represents good value, converting between employee and contractor cost models.

## Contractor & Freelancer Management

**Statement of Work (SOW) as Operating Agreement.** Every contractor engagement, no matter how small, should have a written SOW. The SOW defines: the scope of work (what is included and, critically, what is excluded), deliverables (specific outputs with acceptance criteria), timeline (start date, milestones, end date), rate and payment terms (when and how they get paid), IP ownership (in Australia, contractors generally retain IP in their work unless the contract explicitly assigns it — this is the opposite of employment, where the employer typically owns work product), confidentiality obligations, and termination provisions (notice period, kill fee if applicable). A good SOW protects both parties and eliminates the ambiguity that causes most contractor relationships to sour. Write it in plain English, not legalese — the goal is mutual understanding, not legal intimidation. Have a lawyer review your template once, then reuse it. Use for: every contractor engagement, from a two-day design sprint to a six-month development project.

**Milestone-Based Payment and Quality Gates.** Never pay a contractor 100% upfront or 100% on completion. Structure payments around milestones — defined checkpoints where a deliverable is reviewed and accepted before the next tranche of payment is released. A typical structure for a $20,000 project: 20% on commencement, 30% on delivery of first draft or prototype, 30% on delivery of final version, 20% on acceptance after a review period. Each milestone has a quality gate — a set of criteria the deliverable must meet before payment is triggered. Quality gates should be defined in the SOW and should be objective (passes these tests, meets these specifications) rather than subjective (I like it). This structure aligns incentives: the contractor is motivated to hit milestones to unlock payment, and you have natural checkpoints to course-correct before too much time or money is spent. Use for: any project-based contractor engagement, especially where the total value exceeds a few thousand dollars.

**Async-First Collaboration.** If you are working with contractors across time zones — common in Australia, where you might engage someone in the Philippines, Eastern Europe, or the US — synchronous meetings become expensive coordination overhead. Design your collaboration model to be async-first: written briefs (not verbal walkthroughs), recorded Loom videos for complex explanations, shared project boards for status visibility, and a defined response-time expectation (e.g., "respond to messages within 24 hours during your business days"). Reserve synchronous meetings for kickoffs, complex problem-solving, and relationship-building — not for status updates, which should always be written. The discipline of async-first forces you to be a better communicator: you cannot rely on "I'll just explain it on a call" as a crutch for unclear thinking. Tools that support this: Linear or Notion for project tracking, Loom for async video, Slack with clear channel conventions. Use for: any remote contractor relationship, especially cross-timezone.

**The Contractor Feedback Loop.** Contractors rarely get meaningful feedback, which means they rarely improve. Build a simple feedback loop: at the end of each milestone or at regular intervals (fortnightly for ongoing engagements), share specific feedback on what worked and what needs to change. Use the radical candor framework — care personally, challenge directly. A contractor who receives clear, honest feedback and improves is worth far more than constantly cycling through new people. Also build in a reverse feedback loop: ask the contractor what you could do better as a client. Are your briefs clear? Are you responsive enough? Is the scope well-defined? The best contractor relationships are partnerships, and partnerships require two-way communication. Use for: every contractor engagement lasting more than two weeks, every milestone review.
