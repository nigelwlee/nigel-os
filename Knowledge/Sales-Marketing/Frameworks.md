# Sales & Marketing Frameworks

Mental models and tools for acquiring and retaining customers. Reference when positioning products, planning GTM, running sales conversations, or setting pricing.

---

## Positioning & Messaging

**Obviously Awesome — Dunford's Five-Step Positioning Method.** April Dunford's framework treats positioning not as a tagline exercise but as a strategic decision that cascades into every aspect of go-to-market. The five components are: (1) Competitive Alternatives — what would customers use if your product did not exist, including manual processes, spreadsheets, hiring someone, or a completely different category of tool; (2) Unique Attributes — the capabilities or features only your product has that the alternatives lack; (3) Value — the tangible benefit those unique attributes enable for the customer, expressed in their language, not yours; (4) Target Customer Segments — the buyers for whom that value matters most urgently; (5) Market Category — the context you place your product in so that the value becomes immediately obvious. The method is executed as a team exercise working through each component in order, because changing your competitive alternative redefines what counts as unique, which redefines your value claim. Most positioning failures happen because founders pick a market category first and then try to retrofit differentiation, when they should let their genuine strengths dictate the category. Use for: launching a new product, repositioning after initial traction stalls, writing landing page copy, preparing an investor narrative, briefing a freelance copywriter.

**Schwartz's Five Levels of Awareness — Matching Message to Buyer Stage.** Eugene Schwartz's 1966 framework from *Breakthrough Advertising* remains the most useful model for deciding what to say based on where the buyer is mentally. The five levels are: (1) Unaware — the person does not know they have a problem; (2) Problem-Aware — they feel the pain but do not know solutions exist; (3) Solution-Aware — they know solutions exist but have not found yours; (4) Product-Aware — they know your product but have not bought; (5) Most Aware — they know your product and just need a reason to act now. The practical implication is that your messaging must meet the buyer at their current level. Advertising to unaware audiences with product features is wasted spend — you need to lead with the problem. Advertising to most-aware audiences with educational content is also wasted — you need a clear call to action and an offer. For a solo builder, the most common mistake is writing landing pages that assume product-awareness (feature lists, comparison tables) when the majority of traffic is only problem-aware and needs to see their struggle described before they care about your solution. Use for: writing landing pages, structuring email sequences, deciding on ad creative angles, segmenting content strategy by funnel stage.

**Ogilvy's "Big Idea" — Concept-Driven Creative.** David Ogilvy argued that advertising without a big idea is like a ship without a sail — it will drift and never arrive. The big idea is not a clever headline; it is a concept that is immediately interesting, makes the reader want to know more, and is inherently tied to the product's value. Ogilvy's test: does the idea make you gasp when you first see it? Could it run for twenty years without getting tired? For solo builders, the application is in content marketing and brand narrative. Most founder content is feature-focused or insight-focused but lacks a unifying idea that ties it all together. The founders who build real audiences — the ones whose content gets shared — typically have one big idea that they return to repeatedly from different angles. This is not about being creative for its own sake; it is about having a point of view so clear and surprising that it becomes your brand. Use for: developing a content thesis, creating a brand narrative, evaluating whether a marketing campaign has a strong enough concept, differentiating in a crowded market where features are similar.

**Category Design — Creating a New Market Category.** Popularized by the book *Play Bigger*, category design argues that the company which defines a new category typically captures 76% of the economics of that category. Rather than competing in an existing category (where you are compared on features and price), you define a new problem, name it, and evangelize it until the market accepts the frame. The framework involves identifying a "missing" that the market has not yet articulated, giving it a name, creating a point of view around why this missing matters now, and building a "lightning strike" — a coordinated launch moment that establishes you as the category leader. For AI products, this is especially relevant because many AI tools genuinely do something that has no prior category. The risk is that creating a category requires sustained evangelism, which is expensive in time and attention. A solo builder should only pursue category design if the product truly does not fit an existing category and if they have a credible platform (audience, media relationships, community presence) to drive the narrative. Use when: your product does something genuinely new that cannot be easily compared to existing solutions, when competing in an established category means you will always be seen as the inferior alternative, or when the existing category carries baggage that hurts your positioning.

## Demand Generation

**Dark Social and Demand Creation vs. Demand Capture (Chris Walker).** Walker's framework draws a hard line between demand creation (generating awareness and desire in people who are not yet looking for a solution) and demand capture (intercepting people who are already searching). Demand capture channels — Google Ads, SEO, G2 reviews, comparison landing pages — are high-intent but finite; there are only so many people searching for your category at any given time. Demand creation channels — podcasts, social media content, community participation, events, word-of-mouth — expand the total addressable pipeline by educating the market about a problem and your approach to solving it. The critical insight is that most demand creation happens in channels that traditional marketing attribution cannot track: private Slack groups, DMs, conversations at events, podcast listening. Walker calls this "dark social." The implication for a solo builder is to stop optimizing exclusively for what analytics can measure and start investing in activities that build genuine expertise and trust in the communities where buyers spend time, even if the ROI is not immediately trackable. Use for: deciding how to allocate limited marketing time, choosing between content strategies (SEO-optimized blog posts vs. original-thought LinkedIn posts), understanding why pipeline seems to appear "out of nowhere," justifying investment in brand and community.

**Content-Market Fit — Publishing What Your Buyers Actually Want.** Analogous to product-market fit, content-market fit exists when you are consistently producing content that your target buyers seek out, consume, and share — not because of algorithmic distribution but because it is genuinely useful or perspective-shifting. The framework involves three steps: (1) identify the specific questions, frustrations, and debates your ideal customer has (through sales calls, community lurking, and support tickets); (2) create content that addresses those topics with a point of view, not just information; (3) distribute through the channels where those buyers already pay attention. The most common failure mode is creating content for other practitioners in your own field (builders writing for builders) rather than for the actual buyer (the operations manager, the legal team lead, the marketing director). A solo builder should audit their last ten pieces of content and ask: would my ideal paying customer find this useful? If the answer is no, the content strategy needs to shift. Use for: planning a content calendar, evaluating whether content efforts are attracting buyers or just peers, deciding what to write about when you have limited publishing bandwidth.

**Inbound vs. Outbound — Choosing and Sequencing Acquisition Channels.** Inbound (content, SEO, community, word-of-mouth) and outbound (cold email, LinkedIn outreach, paid ads) are not opposing strategies — they are complementary, but they have different time horizons and economics. Inbound compounds over time: a blog post or a YouTube video can generate leads for years, but it takes months to build enough content for meaningful traffic. Outbound produces results immediately but does not compound: the moment you stop sending emails, pipeline stops. For a solo builder, the optimal sequence is typically: start with outbound and founder-led sales to generate immediate revenue and learn the customer's language, then reinvest learnings into inbound content that scales. The mistake is trying to do both at full intensity simultaneously with no team — you end up doing both poorly. Pick one as your primary motion based on your current stage (pre-revenue = outbound; post-revenue with some traction = begin layering inbound). Use for: deciding where to focus acquisition efforts at each stage, planning the transition from founder-led outbound to scalable inbound, setting realistic expectations for time-to-results on content marketing.

**Community-Led Growth — Turning Users into a Distribution Channel.** Community-led growth builds an audience of practitioners around a shared problem or practice area, not around your product. The community becomes a distribution channel because members trust it, contribute to it, and naturally discover your product through participation. This is distinct from having a "community" that is really just a support forum — true community-led growth means the community has value independent of your product. For AI builders, this often takes the form of a Slack group, Discord, or forum focused on the workflow your product improves (e.g., a community about legal document automation, not a community about your specific tool). The community gives you ongoing access to customer problems, language, and feedback while simultaneously building trust and awareness. The cost is real: communities require consistent moderation, content, and engagement. A solo builder should only pursue this if they genuinely enjoy facilitating conversations and can commit to showing up consistently for at least six months. Use for: building a sustainable acquisition channel that compounds, generating product feedback at scale, creating a moat against competitors who can copy features but cannot copy community trust.

## Sales Process

**Four Forces of Progress (Bob Moesta) — Understanding Why Buyers Switch.** Moesta's demand-side framework models every purchase decision as a struggle between four forces. Two forces push toward the purchase: the "push" of the current situation (frustration, pain, dissatisfaction with the status quo) and the "pull" of the new solution (attraction to a better outcome, excitement about what is possible). Two forces push against the purchase: the "anxiety of the new" (fear of the unknown, implementation risk, "what if it does not work?") and the "habit of the present" (comfort with current workflows, sunk cost in existing tools, inertia). A deal closes when push + pull > anxiety + habit. The practical application in sales conversations is to stop pitching (which only increases pull) and start investigating which forces are active. If a prospect has high anxiety, no amount of feature explanation will close the deal — you need case studies, guarantees, and easy onboarding. If they have low push, they are not going to buy regardless of how good your product is — they do not feel the pain enough. Use for: structuring discovery calls around understanding the buyer's forces, diagnosing why deals stall (usually anxiety or habit, not lack of pull), designing proposals that address specific objections, writing case studies that activate push and reduce anxiety.

**SPIN Selling (Neil Rackham) — Question-Based Discovery for Complex Sales.** SPIN is an acronym for four types of questions that guide a prospect from passive interest to active commitment: Situation questions (gather facts about the prospect's current state), Problem questions (explore difficulties and frustrations), Implication questions (develop the consequences of not solving the problem), and Need-Payoff questions (get the prospect to articulate the value of a solution in their own words). The framework was derived from observing thousands of real sales calls and finding that top performers ask more implication and need-payoff questions than average performers, who tend to get stuck on situation and problem questions. For founder-led sales, SPIN is especially useful because it gives a structured sequence for conversations that avoids the two most common founder mistakes: jumping straight to a demo (skipping discovery entirely) or asking so many questions that the call feels like an interrogation without direction. The key is to move through the sequence naturally, spending most time on implication questions, which is where the prospect convinces themselves that the problem is worth solving. Use for: preparing discovery call scripts, training yourself to sell through questions rather than presentations, qualifying whether a prospect has a problem urgent enough to pay to solve.

**Sandler Selling System — Disqualification and Mutual Agreements.** The Sandler method is built on the principle that selling is a mutual evaluation, not a persuasion exercise. The core mechanic is the "upfront contract" — at the start of every conversation, both parties agree on what will happen, how long it will take, and what the possible outcomes are (including "this is not a fit, and that is fine"). This removes the dynamic where the seller is chasing and the buyer is evading. Sandler also emphasizes disqualification over qualification: instead of trying to convince every prospect, you actively look for reasons the deal will not work (no budget, no authority, no timeline, no pain) and surface them early. For solo builders, this is liberating because it eliminates the emotional toll of chasing prospects who were never going to buy. It also shortens sales cycles because problems that would have killed the deal in month three get surfaced in week one. The framework includes a "pain funnel" — a series of increasingly specific questions that move from surface-level complaints to the real business impact. Use for: structuring sales conversations to protect your time, setting expectations with prospects at the start of every call, disqualifying bad-fit leads early, reducing the emotional burden of founder-led sales.

**Founder-Led Sales Playbook — Selling Before You Have a Sales Team.** This is not a single framework but a pattern observed across successful early-stage companies. The playbook has four components: (1) the founder does all selling personally until at least $500K-$1M ARR, because no one else can iterate on positioning, pricing, and product as fast as the person who builds it; (2) every sales conversation is also a product research conversation — you take notes on the buyer's language, objections, and workflow and feed it directly into product development; (3) the sales process is kept radically simple — typically a two-call structure (discovery + demo/proposal) with no elaborate sequences or stages; (4) the founder documents everything — call recordings, objection patterns, winning talk tracks — so that when they eventually hire a salesperson, there is a playbook to hand over rather than tribal knowledge. The biggest trap for technical founders is treating sales as beneath them or rushing to hire a salesperson before they understand the sales motion themselves. The second biggest trap is never codifying what works, so the first sales hire fails because they cannot replicate the founder's intuition. Use for: structuring your first sales process, deciding when to hire a first salesperson, building a knowledge base of buyer objections and winning responses, transitioning from founder-led to team-led sales.

## Pricing & Packaging

**Value Metric Pricing (Kyle Poyar / OpenView) — Pricing on What Matters.** The value metric is the unit of consumption that most closely tracks the value your customer receives. For a document automation tool, it might be documents processed. For a data platform, it might be rows analyzed. For a collaboration tool, it might be active users. Poyar's framework argues that your pricing should scale along this metric, so that customers who get more value pay more and customers just getting started pay less. This aligns incentives: you succeed when your customer succeeds. The implementation involves identifying 3-5 candidate value metrics, testing them against three criteria (is it easy to understand? does it scale with value? is it predictable enough that customers can estimate their bill?), and then structuring tiers around the winning metric. The most common mistake for AI products is pricing on API calls or tokens, which are cost metrics rather than value metrics — the customer does not care about tokens, they care about documents processed or hours saved. Use for: choosing your pricing axis, designing tier boundaries, evaluating whether your current pricing scales correctly with customer value, preparing for pricing page redesigns.

**Freemium vs. Free Trial — Choosing the Right Free Model.** These are fundamentally different strategies often conflated. Freemium gives permanent access to a limited version of the product; the free tier is a feature of the business model, not a promotion. Free trial gives temporary access to the full product; it is a sales tactic with a built-in deadline. Freemium works best when: the product delivers value immediately with no setup, the free tier naturally generates word-of-mouth or viral loops, and the marginal cost of free users is near zero. Free trial works best when: the product requires configuration or behavior change before value is realized, you need urgency to drive activation, and the full product experience is necessary to demonstrate value. For AI products with a learning curve, a free trial is almost always superior because it creates time pressure that motivates the user to invest effort in setup, whereas a free tier allows infinite procrastination. The hybrid model — a freemium tier plus a trial of premium features — can work but adds complexity that a solo builder rarely needs. Use for: deciding between free models at launch, redesigning a free tier that is not converting, evaluating whether your free users are a growth engine or a cost center.

**Price Anchoring and Tier Psychology — Structuring Choices to Guide Decisions.** Behavioral pricing research consistently shows that people do not evaluate prices in absolute terms but relative to nearby options. The practical application is the three-tier structure: a low tier that exists to make the middle tier look reasonable, a middle tier that is where you want most customers to land, and a high tier that anchors the perceived value upward and captures willingness to pay from power users. The middle tier should be the obvious "best deal" — it should offer disproportionately more value per dollar than the low tier. The high tier should include at least one feature that justifies a significant price premium (dedicated support, custom integrations, SLA) even if very few customers choose it. For solo builders, the common mistake is offering only one plan or two plans. A single plan forces a binary yes/no decision. Two plans create comparison but no anchor. Three plans create a choice architecture that nudges toward the middle. Use for: designing your pricing page, structuring plan features to encourage upgrades, setting the price of a premium tier, running pricing experiments.

**Willingness-to-Pay Research (Van Westendorp / Gabor-Granger) — Getting Pricing Data Before Launch.** These are two survey-based methods for estimating price sensitivity before you commit to a pricing structure. Van Westendorp asks four questions: at what price is the product too expensive (you would never consider it), at what price is it expensive but you would still consider it, at what price is it a bargain, and at what price is it so cheap you would question its quality? Plotting the responses reveals an acceptable price range and an optimal price point. Gabor-Granger is simpler: show a price and ask "would you buy at this price?" then adjust up or down. Both methods have limitations — survey responses do not perfectly predict real purchasing behavior — but they are dramatically better than guessing. For a solo builder, running these surveys with 20-30 people from your target segment takes a few hours and can prevent months of revenue loss from mispricing. The output is not a final price but a range that brackets your decision and identifies psychological thresholds (e.g., $49 vs. $50 may have very different acceptance rates). Use for: setting initial pricing before launch, validating a planned price increase, identifying the price band where demand drops off, gathering evidence to support pricing decisions with co-founders or advisors.

## Metrics & Analytics

**Pirate Metrics / AARRR (Dave McClure) — The Funnel as a Diagnostic Tool.** McClure's framework breaks the customer journey into five stages: Acquisition (how do users find you?), Activation (do they have a good first experience?), Retention (do they come back?), Revenue (do they pay?), and Referral (do they tell others?). The power of the framework is not in the stages themselves but in using them as a diagnostic: measure conversion rates between each stage, identify the biggest drop-off, and focus all effort there. For a solo builder, the most common mistake is obsessing over acquisition (more traffic, more sign-ups) when the real problem is activation (users sign up but never complete a meaningful action) or retention (users activate but do not return in week two). The framework enforces discipline: fix the stage with the worst conversion rate before investing in the stages above it, because pouring more traffic into a broken funnel is waste. Use for: auditing your funnel to find the biggest leverage point, deciding where to focus product and marketing effort, structuring analytics dashboards, prioritizing feature work by funnel impact.

**LTV/CAC Ratio — The Unit Economics Health Check.** Lifetime Value (LTV) divided by Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC) is the single most important metric for a subscription business. The general benchmark is that LTV/CAC should be above 3:1 — meaning a customer generates at least three times the revenue of what it cost to acquire them. Below 3:1, you are likely spending too much on acquisition or not retaining customers long enough. Above 5:1, you may be under-investing in growth (you could afford to spend more to grow faster). For a solo builder, the trap is calculating LTV optimistically (assuming low churn forever) and CAC narrowly (only counting ad spend, ignoring the cost of your own time). Honest LTV/CAC calculation requires including your time as a cost, using actual observed churn rates (not projections), and accounting for all acquisition costs including content production, tooling, and any freelancer spend. The metric is most useful as a trend line — is it improving or degrading over time? — rather than as an absolute number. Use for: evaluating whether your growth is sustainable, deciding whether to increase or decrease acquisition spending, benchmarking your business health, preparing for investor conversations about unit economics.

**Cohort Analysis — Seeing Retention Through Time.** Cohort analysis groups customers by their sign-up date (or another shared characteristic) and tracks their behavior over time. The output is typically a retention table: of the customers who signed up in January, what percentage were still active in February, March, April, and so on? This is dramatically more useful than looking at aggregate retention numbers, which mix new and old users and can mask deterioration. The key patterns to look for: a "flattening curve" where retention stabilizes after an initial drop (healthy — you have found a core user base), a "declining curve" where retention never stabilizes (dangerous — you have a leaky bucket), or an "improving curve" where newer cohorts retain better than older ones (positive signal that product improvements are working). For AI products specifically, cohort analysis can also reveal whether retention correlates with usage intensity in the first week, which helps you design activation interventions. Use for: understanding true retention beyond vanity metrics, evaluating whether product changes improve long-term engagement, identifying which user segments retain best, making decisions about where to invest in the product.

**Leading vs. Lagging Indicators — Measuring What You Can Still Influence.** Revenue, churn, and MRR are lagging indicators — by the time they change, the underlying cause happened weeks or months ago. Leading indicators are the upstream behaviors that predict those outcomes: activation rate (predicts retention), support ticket volume (predicts churn), feature adoption depth (predicts expansion revenue), NPS or CSAT scores (predict word-of-mouth growth). The framework requires identifying which leading indicators most strongly correlate with your lagging outcomes, then building dashboards and alerts around those leading metrics. For a solo builder, the practical implementation is simple: pick two or three leading indicators that you check weekly (e.g., percentage of new users who complete their first output within 24 hours, number of users who used the product three or more times this week, number of inbound inquiries from non-paid channels) and use those to make decisions rather than waiting for monthly revenue numbers to tell you something is wrong. The most dangerous situation is when lagging metrics look fine but leading indicators are declining — this means trouble is coming but has not arrived yet. Use for: building a weekly metrics review habit, creating early warning systems for churn or growth slowdowns, focusing product effort on behaviors that drive outcomes rather than outcomes directly.
