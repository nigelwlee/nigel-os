# Creative Frameworks

Mental models and tools for creative work — writing, brand, design, content. Reference when briefing, creating, editing, or evaluating creative output.

---

## Briefing & Direction

**The Creative Brief (Minimal Version)**
Five questions that must be answered before any creative work starts:
1. What is the single job this work must do? (SOCO)
2. Who is it for? (Specific person, not demographic)
3. What do they currently believe? What should they believe after?
4. What proof do we have that earns that belief shift?
5. What does success look like? (Measurable, not "impactful")

**The Reference Library**
Collect 5-10 references before briefing visual or written work. Organise by: tone references (what feeling), style references (what look), anti-references (what to avoid). The anti-references are often more useful than the references.

**The Single Sentence Test**
Can you describe the creative work in one sentence that a 12-year-old would understand? If not, the concept is too complex or not yet resolved. Use for: pitching ideas, testing clarity, editing briefs.

## Writing

**Pyramid Structure for Copy**
Most important information first. Each paragraph's first sentence is the most important. Reader can stop anywhere and still have the core message. Opposite of academic writing. Use for: emails, headlines, product copy, announcements.

**Show Don't Tell**
"She was nervous" is telling. "She checked her phone three times in two minutes" is showing. The specific, observable detail creates more emotional effect than the label. Use in: all narrative writing, case studies, testimonials.

**The Rule of Three**
Three is the minimum number of examples that feels convincing. Two feels thin; four feels tedious. "Fast, simple, and powerful" works. "Fast, simple, powerful, and flexible" loses a beat. Use for: copywriting, presentations, pitches.

**Active Voice Default**
"The team shipped the feature" > "The feature was shipped by the team." Active voice is almost always shorter, clearer, and more energetic. Flag passive voice on every pass.

**Hemingway Edit**
Read your draft and cut anything you don't need. Then cut again. The test: if removing a word or sentence loses nothing, cut it. Aim for the minimum word count that preserves all meaning.

## Design & Visual

**Visual Hierarchy**
Every design has one thing the eye should see first, second, third. If everything is emphasised, nothing is. Use: size, weight, colour, spacing, and contrast to establish hierarchy. Test by squinting at the design — what do you still read?

**Whitespace as a Tool**
Whitespace is not empty space — it is a design element that creates emphasis, pace, and breathing room. Tight whitespace = density and urgency. Generous whitespace = luxury and calm. Match to brand positioning.

**The 60-30-10 Colour Rule**
60% dominant colour (usually neutral/background), 30% secondary colour (structural), 10% accent (emphasis). Prevents visual chaos. Use as a starting constraint, break intentionally.

**Gestalt Principles (Applied)**
- Proximity: things close together appear related
- Similarity: things that look alike appear related
- Continuity: the eye follows lines and curves
- Closure: the brain completes incomplete shapes
Use when: laying out information, deciding what to group, explaining why something feels "off."

## Brand Strategy

**Brand Archetypes (Jung)**
12 archetypes: Hero, Outlaw, Explorer, Sage, Innocent, Creator, Ruler, Magician, Lover, Caregiver, Jester, Everyman. Each has a consistent set of values, fears, and communication style. Use for: establishing brand personality, ensuring consistency across touchpoints.

**The Brand Person Exercise**
Answer: Who is this brand as a person? Age, profession, personality, style, values, what they read, what they'd never say. The more specific the person, the more useful the exercise. Use for: voice and tone, visual direction, deciding what content to make.

**Category Entry Points (Sharp, Byron)**
Customers think of brands in buying situations, not in the abstract. Define the mental associations you want to own at the moment of purchase. Use for: positioning, media strategy, content strategy.

## Content Strategy

**Content Pillars**
Define 3-5 recurring themes that all content falls into. Each pillar serves a purpose: authority, community, conversion, entertainment. Content that doesn't fit a pillar doesn't get made. Use for: editorial planning, social strategy, maintaining consistency.

**The 80/20 Content Rule**
80% of content should deliver value with no ask. 20% can convert or promote. Reversing this ratio burns trust fast. Use for: social, email, any owned channel.

**Repurposing Ladder**
One long-form piece (article, podcast, talk) → 3-5 medium pieces (threads, clips, summaries) → 10+ short pieces (quotes, stats, images). Create once, distribute many. Use for: content efficiency, maintaining output without proportional time investment.
