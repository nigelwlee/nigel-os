# Finance Frameworks

Mental models and tools for financial thinking. Reference when evaluating unit economics, planning runway, fundraising, or making investment decisions.

---

## Unit Economics & Metrics

**Lifetime Value (LTV).** LTV represents the total revenue (or gross profit) you expect to earn from a single customer over the entire duration of their relationship with your product. The simplest formulation is LTV = ARPU / Churn Rate, where ARPU is average revenue per user per month and churn is monthly churn rate. For a more accurate picture, use gross-margin-adjusted LTV: multiply by your gross margin percentage so you are measuring the actual profit contribution, not just revenue. The critical nuance for AI products: if your COGS scales with usage (API calls, inference compute), your gross margin may vary significantly across customer segments, which means you need segment-level LTV calculations, not a single blended number. A common mistake is calculating LTV on revenue rather than gross profit, which makes your economics look 20-40% better than they actually are. At early stage with limited data, use a conservative assumption of 12-18 month customer lifetime rather than extrapolating from a tiny sample. Use for: pricing decisions, determining how much you can spend on acquisition, evaluating whether a customer segment is worth pursuing, investor conversations about business model health.

**Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC) and the LTV:CAC Ratio.** CAC is the fully loaded cost of acquiring a single new customer: total sales and marketing spend (including your time valued at a reasonable rate, paid ads, content production, tools) divided by new customers acquired in the same period. The LTV:CAC ratio is the primary indicator of business model viability. Below 1:1 you are destroying value with every customer. Between 1:1 and 3:1 you are in a marginal zone where the business can work but has little room for error. Above 3:1 is healthy, and above 5:1 either means you have exceptional product-market fit or you are under-investing in growth. For a solo builder, the biggest trap is ignoring the cost of your own time in CAC. If you spend 20 hours per week on sales and marketing, that is real cost even if you are not paying yourself. The payback period — how many months of revenue it takes to recover CAC — is equally important: anything over 12 months for an early-stage company means you need significant capital to fund the gap between acquisition cost and revenue recovery. Use for: setting marketing budgets, evaluating channel efficiency (calculate CAC per channel, not just blended), deciding whether to invest in growth or retention, fundraising narratives.

**Monthly Recurring Revenue (MRR) and Annual Recurring Revenue (ARR).** MRR is the normalized monthly revenue from all active subscriptions, excluding one-time fees, professional services, and variable usage above contracted minimums. ARR is simply MRR multiplied by 12. These are the foundational metrics of any subscription business because they represent the predictable, recurring component of revenue — which is what investors value and what you can plan against. The decomposition of MRR changes is where the real insight lives: break it into New MRR (from new customers), Expansion MRR (upgrades, seat additions, usage growth from existing customers), Contraction MRR (downgrades), and Churned MRR (lost customers). This decomposition tells you whether growth is coming from acquisition or expansion, and whether you have a retention problem masked by strong top-of-funnel. For AI products with usage-based pricing, deciding what counts as "recurring" vs. "variable" requires careful definition. Use for: tracking business health weekly/monthly, investor reporting, financial planning, understanding growth composition.

**Gross Margin Decomposition.** Gross margin is revenue minus cost of goods sold (COGS), expressed as a percentage of revenue. For software businesses, COGS typically includes hosting/infrastructure, third-party API costs (critical for AI products using OpenAI, Anthropic, etc.), payment processing fees, and any direct customer support costs. The framework is to decompose gross margin into its components and track each independently over time. For AI builders, the most important sub-component is inference cost per customer per month — this can be 15-40% of revenue for products that make heavy use of LLM APIs, which drags gross margin from the typical SaaS 80%+ down into the 50-70% range. Understanding this decomposition is essential for pricing: if your inference cost per query is $0.03 and a customer makes 1,000 queries per month, that is $30/month in pure COGS before anything else. Pricing at $50/month gives you a 40% gross margin on that component alone, which is not a software business — it is a services business wearing a software costume. Use for: pricing architecture, identifying where to invest in cost reduction (caching, model distillation, prompt optimisation), understanding whether you are building a high-margin or low-margin business, evaluating build-vs-buy for infrastructure.

## Cash Flow & Runway

**Burn Rate (Gross and Net).** Gross burn is your total monthly cash outflows — every dollar leaving the business, including salaries, rent, tools, infrastructure, marketing, and your personal draw. Net burn is gross burn minus revenue: the actual monthly cash consumption of the business. The distinction matters because gross burn tells you your cost structure (and therefore what you need to cut in an emergency), while net burn tells you how fast your cash is actually depleting. For a solo builder, the discipline is tracking both weekly in a simple spreadsheet. Common items that solo builders forget to include: personal draw or living expenses funded by the business, annual subscriptions (amortise monthly), tax obligations accruing but not yet paid (GST, income tax, super contributions), and one-off costs that recur more often than you think. A useful stress test: calculate your "emergency burn" — the absolute minimum you need to keep the business alive and yourself fed — because knowing this number tells you your maximum runway in a crisis. Use for: weekly cash monitoring, scenario planning, deciding when to cut costs vs. invest, communicating financial position to advisors or investors.

**Runway Calculation and Thresholds.** Runway = total available cash divided by monthly net burn rate. This is the most important number in your business until you are cash-flow positive. The framework is to maintain a rolling runway calculation with three scenarios: base case (current trajectory), optimistic (include committed but not yet received revenue, or expected R&D tax refund), and pessimistic (assume revenue drops 30% and costs stay flat). The thresholds are: 18+ months means you are in strategic mode and can afford patience and experimentation; 12-18 months is building mode where you should be focused on the one or two things that will either generate revenue or raise capital; 6-12 months is action mode where you need a clear plan to either reach profitability or close a funding round; below 6 months is emergency mode where you should be cutting everything non-essential and having urgent conversations about capital. A critical nuance for AU-based builders: if you are expecting an R&D Tax Incentive refund, do not count it in your base case until the ATO has assessed your return — refunds can take 30-90 days after lodgement and can be delayed by review or audit. Use for: weekly financial check-in, determining when to start fundraising (always start at 9-12 months of runway, never at 6), making hire/no-hire decisions, deciding whether to take on a project or client for cash flow.

**Cash Conversion Cycle.** The cash conversion cycle measures the time between when you spend money (on development, marketing, sales) and when you receive cash from customers. For SaaS businesses with monthly billing, this can be as short as 30 days, but for annual contracts paid in arrears, enterprise deals with 60-90 day payment terms, or businesses that invest heavily upfront in content or product before monetising, it can stretch to 6-12 months. The framework is to map out your specific cash conversion cycle: (1) time to build/create, (2) time to sell, (3) time from sale to first payment, (4) payment terms. Each stage is a lever you can pull. Offering annual prepaid plans with a discount compresses the cycle dramatically — a 15-20% discount for annual upfront payment is almost always worth it because the time value of having that cash now (in terms of runway and optionality) exceeds the discount cost. For AU businesses selling internationally, currency conversion timing and FX fees add another dimension. Use for: designing pricing and payment structures, deciding between monthly and annual billing, managing working capital, understanding why you might be growing revenue but still running low on cash.

**Working Capital Management for Solo Operators.** Working capital is current assets minus current liabilities — essentially, the cash and near-cash you have available to fund day-to-day operations. For a solo builder, this simplifies to: cash in bank plus receivables minus payables minus accrued obligations (GST owing, estimated tax, unpaid subscriptions). The framework is to maintain a simple working capital dashboard updated weekly that includes: bank balance, outstanding invoices (aged by 30/60/90 days), upcoming committed expenses (next 90 days), and accrued tax obligations. The most common working capital trap for solo builders is the GST/tax timing mismatch: you collect GST on sales and it sits in your bank account looking like available cash, but it belongs to the ATO. Similarly, if you are on PAYG instalments or have a projected tax liability, that cash is spoken for even though it is technically in your account. Ring-fencing tax obligations in a separate account (or at minimum tracking them separately) prevents the painful surprise of a BAS or tax bill that wipes out what you thought was 3 months of runway. Use for: weekly financial health check, avoiding cash crunches from tax obligations, managing the timing of large purchases, deciding when you can afford to invest in growth.

## Fundraising & Capital

**SAFEs and Convertible Notes (AU Context).** A SAFE (Simple Agreement for Future Equity) is an instrument where an investor provides capital now in exchange for the right to convert that investment into equity at a future priced round, typically at a discount or subject to a valuation cap. A convertible note is similar but structured as debt with an interest rate and maturity date, converting into equity at a qualifying event. In the US (YC ecosystem), SAFEs are standard. In Australia, the landscape is more complex. Australian Pty Ltd structures do not have the same class-of-shares flexibility as Delaware C-Corps, so SAFEs need to be adapted. AVCAL (Australian Investment Council) has published AU-specific convertible note and SAFE-equivalent templates. Key AU considerations: (1) a SAFE may be treated as a financial product under the Corporations Act, triggering disclosure obligations unless an exemption applies (typically the small-scale offering exemption, limited to 20 investors in 12 months); (2) the accounting treatment is ambiguous — it could be debt or equity on your balance sheet, which affects your R&D Tax Incentive eligibility calculation; (3) interest on convertible notes is deductible for the company but taxable for the investor, creating different incentive structures than US equivalents. Use for: raising pre-seed or seed capital, structuring angel investments, understanding what instrument to offer AU vs. US investors.

**Dilution Mechanics and Cap Table Modelling.** Dilution is the reduction in your ownership percentage when new shares are issued. The framework is to model your cap table forward through multiple rounds with realistic assumptions. Start with your founding equity (100% if solo), then model: (1) an option pool (typically 10-15% set aside before or at first institutional round), (2) seed round (15-25% dilution is typical), (3) Series A (15-25% dilution), and so on. The compounding effect is what most founders underestimate: if you give away 20% at seed, 20% at Series A, and 20% at Series B, you do not own 40% — you own 51.2% (0.8 x 0.8 x 0.8). With an option pool carved out, you might be at 40-45% after a Series A. The practical implication for a solo founder: every percentage point matters more when there is no co-founder to share the dilution with. This makes capital efficiency (raising less, proving more before raising) disproportionately important. Model your cap table with a tool like Carta, Pulley, or even a spreadsheet, and run scenarios before any capital conversation. Use for: deciding how much to raise (raise what you need for 18-24 months, not more), evaluating term sheets, understanding the real cost of giving away equity to early hires or advisors, negotiating valuation caps on SAFEs.

**Term Sheet Economics vs. Control.** Following Feld's framework, every term in a financing deal is either an economic term (affecting how money is distributed) or a control term (affecting who makes decisions). The critical economic terms are: pre-money valuation (what the company is worth before investment), liquidation preference (who gets paid first and how much in an exit), participation rights (whether investors double-dip by getting their preference AND sharing in remaining proceeds), and anti-dilution provisions (whether investors get price protection if you raise a down round). The critical control terms are: board composition (who has seats and therefore voting power), protective provisions (investor veto rights over certain actions like raising more capital, selling the company, or changing the business), and information rights. For a solo founder, control terms are arguably more important than economic terms because losing control of your board or having restrictive protective provisions can prevent you from making the fast, unilateral decisions that are your primary advantage as a solo operator. Use for: evaluating any term sheet, understanding what to negotiate hard on (control terms, liquidation preferences) vs. where to be flexible (valuation within a reasonable range), preparing for investor conversations.

**Non-Dilutive Capital Sources (AU Specific).** Before taking dilutive equity capital, exhaust non-dilutive options. In Australia, the primary sources are: (1) the R&D Tax Incentive (43.5% refundable offset for sub-$20M turnover companies, potentially worth $50-150K/year for an AI startup with significant development costs); (2) state and federal grants (the Accelerating Commercialisation program, various state innovation grants, and Export Market Development Grants for international sales); (3) revenue-based financing (providers like Lighter Capital or Clearco who advance capital against future revenue for a fixed fee rather than equity); (4) customer pre-payments (offering annual plans or pilot fees to fund development); and (5) government-backed loans (such as those through the Clean Energy Finance Corporation for climate-adjacent products). The framework is to evaluate each source on four dimensions: (a) amount available, (b) cost (both direct and in compliance/administration time), (c) timing (when you receive the cash), and (d) strings attached (reporting obligations, milestones, clawback provisions). Use for: building a capital strategy before defaulting to equity fundraising, extending runway without dilution, layering multiple capital sources to reduce dependence on any single one.

## Valuation & Modelling

**Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) for Early Stage.** The DCF method values a business as the present value of all future free cash flows, discounted at a rate reflecting the risk of those cash flows. For an early-stage startup, DCF is theoretically correct but practically challenging because you are forecasting cash flows years out with enormous uncertainty. The framework, following Damodaran, is to use DCF not as a precise valuation tool but as a discipline for making your assumptions explicit. Build a simple model: (1) revenue forecast for 5-10 years based on market size, penetration rate, and growth trajectory; (2) operating margin assumption at maturity (benchmark against comparable public companies); (3) reinvestment rate (how much of revenue must be ploughed back to sustain growth); (4) terminal value (what the business is worth at the end of your projection period, typically using an exit multiple); and (5) discount rate (for an early-stage startup, 30-50% is appropriate, reflecting the high probability of failure). The output is less important than the exercise: it forces you to articulate what has to be true for your business to be worth a given valuation. When an investor offers you a $5M pre-money valuation, reverse-engineer what that implies about your future revenue and margins — it is often a reality check. Use for: sanity-checking valuations (your own and investors'), making fundraising assumptions explicit, scenario planning for different growth trajectories.

**Revenue Multiple Benchmarking.** In practice, most early-stage SaaS companies are valued using revenue multiples: Enterprise Value / ARR (or forward revenue). The framework is to understand what drives multiples and where your business sits. As of 2025-26, public SaaS multiples range from 4-8x ARR for average growers to 15-25x+ for high-growth, high-retention businesses. Private early-stage multiples are typically higher (reflecting illiquidity premium inversion — investors pay up for growth potential) and more variable. The key drivers of multiples are: (1) revenue growth rate (the single biggest factor), (2) net revenue retention (above 120% commands premium multiples), (3) gross margin (below 70% compresses multiples significantly), and (4) market narrative (AI companies currently command premium multiples vs. traditional SaaS). For an AU-based company, expect a 20-40% discount to comparable US companies at early stage due to smaller local market, less liquid secondary markets, and currency risk. The practical use: if you are raising at a $5M pre-money on $500K ARR, you are asking for a 10x multiple — is that justified by your growth rate and retention? Use for: evaluating whether a proposed valuation is reasonable, benchmarking against comparable companies, understanding what metrics you need to improve to command a higher valuation, setting price expectations before fundraising conversations.

**Scenario Modelling (Base / Bull / Bear).** Rather than producing a single financial forecast, build three scenarios that represent meaningfully different futures. The base case is your honest best estimate — the plan you actually believe you will execute. The bull case is what happens if things go well: higher conversion rates, faster growth, a key partnership lands, churn is lower than expected. The bear case is what happens if things go poorly: growth stalls, a key customer churns, CAC increases due to competition, or a platform dependency (like an AI API) raises prices. The framework is to define 3-5 key variables that drive your business (growth rate, churn rate, ARPU, CAC, gross margin) and set different assumptions for each across the three scenarios. Then model the financial outcomes (revenue, burn rate, runway, cash position) for each scenario over 12-24 months. The bear case is the most important: it tells you your "survive" scenario and whether you have enough runway and flexibility to weather it. If your bear case leads to running out of cash in 8 months, you need to either raise more capital, cut costs, or de-risk the key variables. Use for: financial planning, board/investor updates, personal decision-making about when to raise or cut, stress-testing your business model.

**Comparable Transaction Analysis.** This framework values your company by reference to what similar companies have been acquired for or raised capital at. The approach: (1) identify 5-10 companies similar to yours in stage, market, and business model; (2) find their most recent valuation events (fundraising rounds via Crunchbase/PitchBook, or acquisitions via press/SEC filings); (3) calculate the implied multiples (EV/Revenue, EV/ARR, price per user); and (4) apply those multiples to your own metrics. The nuances: comparables are never truly comparable (different geographies, growth rates, team sizes, and market conditions), so this is a triangulation tool, not a precision instrument. For AU companies, look at both local comparables (AU/NZ exits and raises) and international ones, but discount international multiples to reflect the AU market dynamics. Recent AU SaaS acquisitions and raises are tracked by platforms like Cut Through Venture and Techboard. Use for: setting valuation expectations before fundraising, negotiating with investors by referencing market data, understanding where the market is pricing companies like yours.

## Tax & Structure (AU Focus)

**R&D Tax Incentive (Detailed Framework).** The R&D Tax Incentive is Australia's primary mechanism for supporting innovation through the tax system. For companies with aggregated turnover under $20M, it provides a 43.5% refundable tax offset on eligible R&D expenditure — meaning you get 43.5 cents back for every dollar of eligible spend, paid as a cash refund even if you have no tax liability. The framework for maximising this: (1) understand eligibility — activities must involve generating new knowledge or information, must be conducted using a systematic progression of work based on established science, and must involve technical uncertainty (i.e., the outcome could not be known in advance by a competent professional); (2) register with AusIndustry within 10 months of your income year end; (3) maintain contemporaneous documentation — lab notebooks, commit logs with technical commentary, design documents explaining hypotheses and experiments, records of iterations and failures; (4) correctly classify expenditure between core R&D activities and supporting activities; (5) be conservative on claims and ensure you can defend every dollar if audited. For AI/software companies, eligible activities typically include: developing novel algorithms or models, experimenting with new architectures, building systems where the technical approach was uncertain, and creating tools to enable R&D. Routine software development, applying known techniques to known problems, and building features where the outcome was predictable are generally not eligible. The $150M expenditure cap and the clawback provisions for companies approaching profitability are worth understanding but are unlikely to apply to an early-stage solo builder. Use for: annual tax planning, structuring development work to maximise eligibility, deciding whether the compliance cost is justified at your scale, engaging R&D tax advisors with informed questions.

**GST for Digital Products and Services.** GST (Goods and Services Tax) at 10% applies to most goods and services sold in Australia. For a solo builder selling digital products, the key rules are: (1) you must register for GST when your GST turnover reaches $75K (this is turnover, not profit); (2) B2B sales to GST-registered businesses are effectively neutral because your customer claims back the GST as an input credit; (3) B2C sales to Australian consumers bear the full 10% — your customer cannot claim it back, so it either reduces your margin or increases your price; (4) exports of digital services to non-Australian residents are generally GST-free, which is a significant advantage for global SaaS products; (5) you can claim GST input credits on your business purchases (hosting, tools, professional services), which partially offsets GST collected. The BAS (Business Activity Statement) is lodged quarterly (or monthly if turnover exceeds $20M or you choose to). Cash vs. accrual accounting for GST purposes matters for timing: cash basis means you account for GST when you receive payment (better for cash flow), accrual basis means you account when you invoice (required above $10M turnover, optional below). Strategic implications: pricing in GST-inclusive terms for B2C simplifies the customer experience but means your effective revenue is 1/11th less than the sticker price. For international sales, maintaining clean records of the customer's location is essential for supporting the GST-free treatment on export. Use for: pricing strategy (B2B vs. B2C, domestic vs. international), cash flow planning around BAS obligations, structuring your business to minimise the GST drag on margins, deciding when to voluntarily register for GST (before the $75K threshold, to claim input credits on setup costs).

**Company Tax and Structure for Solo Builders.** The base rate entity company tax rate in Australia is 25% for companies with aggregated turnover under $50M (and where no more than 80% of assessable income is passive). For a solo builder operating through a Pty Ltd, the framework for thinking about tax efficiency is: (1) salary/wages paid to yourself are deductible to the company but taxable to you at marginal rates (up to 47% including Medicare levy); (2) profits retained in the company are taxed at 25%; (3) dividends paid to you are taxable at your marginal rate but carry franking credits for the 25% company tax already paid; (4) the optimal split between salary and dividends depends on your total income, other deductions, and superannuation obligations. The Div 7A rules are critical: if you withdraw money from your company that is not salary, dividends, or a compliant loan, it will be deemed an unfranked dividend and taxed at your marginal rate with no franking credit — this is the biggest tax trap for solo operator Pty Ltds. The framework for choosing between structures: sole trader is simplest and works below $120K profit; Pty Ltd saves tax above $150K profit and provides asset protection; a discretionary trust (with a Pty Ltd as corporate trustee) provides income-splitting flexibility if you have a partner or family members, but adds $5-8K/year in compliance costs and complexity. Use for: choosing your business structure, optimising the salary/dividend split, avoiding Div 7A pitfalls, annual tax planning conversations with your accountant.

**Employee Share Schemes (ESS) and Options in Australia.** If you are planning to hire or engage contractors and want to offer equity as part of compensation, the ESS rules govern the tax treatment. Since the 2022 reforms, eligible startups (broadly: incorporated for less than 10 years, turnover under $50M, not listed) can offer options to employees at a discount and the taxing point is deferred until a liquidity event (exercise, sale, or expiry, up to a maximum deferral of 15 years) rather than at grant. The framework: (1) use options (rights to acquire shares at a future exercise price) rather than issuing shares directly, as options defer the tax event; (2) ensure the exercise price is at or above market value at grant to qualify for startup concessions; (3) have a formal valuation done (or a defensible methodology) to set the exercise price — the ATO can challenge this; (4) provide ESS statements to participants and lodge the ESS annual report with the ATO. For a solo builder thinking about future hires or advisor compensation: set up an ESOP (Employee Share Option Plan) early, allocate a pool (10-15% is standard), and use a vesting schedule (4-year vest with 1-year cliff is the norm). Getting this structure in place before you need it avoids the pain of trying to retrofit equity incentives into a company that was not set up for them. Note that contractors (not employees) have different and less favourable ESS treatment — this is a common gotcha for startups that rely heavily on contractors. Use for: planning equity compensation for future hires, structuring advisor agreements, understanding the tax implications of issuing equity, setting up an ESOP proactively.
